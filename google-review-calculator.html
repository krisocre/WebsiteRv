How the Google review calculator works

If a business has n reviews averaging a, and receives x new 5-star reviews, the new average is:

New average = (n × a + 5 × x) ÷ (n + x)

Solving for the number of new reviews needed to reach target t gives:

x = n × (t − a) ÷ (5 − t)

The calculator rounds x up to the next whole review because a fraction of a review is not possible. If the current average already meets the target, the answer is zero. If the target is a perfect 5.0 and the current average is below 5.0, no finite number of new 5-star reviews can make the exact mathematical average equal 5.0.

Exact average versus displayed rating

The number shown on a public profile may be rounded to one decimal, while the exact underlying average is not visible. The “exact target” method requires the calculated average to meet the entered number. The “display threshold” method uses a conventional half-step threshold—target minus 0.05—as an estimate of what might round to that one-decimal display.

Why the display estimate can be wrong: the visible current rating is already rounded, the platform’s internal calculation and display updates are not controlled by this page, and review filtering can change the count. Use the exact method for conservative planning and treat both outputs as estimates.

Worked examples

Example: 50 reviews at 4.2, target 4.5

The exact-target calculation is 50 × (4.5 − 4.2) ÷ (5 − 4.5) = 30. If every new review is 5 stars and the displayed inputs equal the exact underlying data, 30 new reviews bring the average to exactly 4.5.

Example: 120 reviews at 4.6, target 4.8

The exact calculation is 120 × 0.2 ÷ 0.2 = 120 new 5-star reviews. Moving a well-established average near five requires many reviews because the new values have limited room above the existing average.

Example: 10 reviews at 4.0, target 4.5

The exact calculation is 10 × 0.5 ÷ 0.5 = 10 new 5-star reviews. Afterward, 40 existing rating points plus 50 new points equal 90 points across 20 reviews, or 4.5.

How to use the target responsibly

A calculator should inform service improvement and authentic request planning—not justify fabricated, paid or incentivized reviews. Invite all eligible genuine customers, ask for honest feedback, and let them choose their own rating and words.

  • Use the number as a planning scenario, not a staff quota.
  • Do not promise the business a particular rating by a particular date.
  • Track customer experience themes as well as the average.
  • Keep the review invitation neutral and incentive-free.
  • Expect legitimate ratings across the whole scale.
  • Recalculate using current numbers rather than hiding older feedback.

Calculator questions

Why does the required number rise so quickly near 5.0?

New reviews cannot score above 5. Each new 5-star rating pulls the average upward by a decreasing amount. An exact average below five can approach 5.0 indefinitely but cannot become exactly 5.0 through a finite number of additional fives.

Can I calculate the effect of a future one-star review?

Use the general weighted-average formula. Add the future star points to the current total and divide by the new count. This calculator focuses on the common five-star planning question and does not predict actual customer ratings.

Does Google weigh some reviews more heavily in the star average?

This calculator assumes a simple arithmetic mean because it is transparent and can be checked. It does not claim access to Google’s internal systems. Display, filtering and product behaviour can change.

Why is the current displayed rating not enough for an exact answer?

A displayed 4.2 might represent a range of underlying averages depending on rounding. Two profiles with the same count and displayed rating can therefore cross the next display threshold after different numbers of reviews.

Can removed reviews change the result?

Yes. If Google removes existing or new reviews, both the total star points and count change. Re-enter the current visible values after any material change.
